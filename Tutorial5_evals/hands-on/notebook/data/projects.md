# Lab projects

A (fictional) freshwater research group runs three projects on microplastics (MPs).
Each week the paper-triage agent reads the new papers, decides which project(s)
each paper is relevant to and at what level, explains how, and sends one digest
grouped by project.
The three projects follow one story: **measure** (P1) → **understand** (P2) → **capture** (P3).

## How to judge relevance

A paper can be relevant to **zero, one, or several** projects. For each project it is:

- **Core** — it directly advances one of the project's aims: new data, a new method,
  model or material for exactly what the project studies.
- **Related** — it does not advance an aim directly, but the project lead would *use* it.
  You must be able to say what they would do with it:
  - **compare** — a competing approach to benchmark against;
  - **borrow** — a method or mechanism from another setting that could be adapted;
  - **cite** — reviews, occurrence data or background the project builds on.
- **Not relevant** — anything else. In particular:
  - the only link is "it is also about microplastics";
  - it shares a *material or keyword* but not the *task* (e.g. biochar used to capture
    cadmium is not related to capturing MPs with biochar);
  - biological or human-health effects of MPs (no project studies effects).

Rule of thumb: *if you cannot name what the project lead would do with the paper, it is
not relevant.*

## P1 — Detecting small microplastics in drinking water

**Goal.** Develop and validate a fast, reliable method to identify and count
microplastic particles of 1–20 µm in tap and bottled water.

**Aims.**
1. *Sample preparation* — filtration and digestion protocols for low-particle
   water that limit contamination and particle loss.
2. *Identification* — Raman / FTIR micro-spectroscopy, Py-GC/MS, automated
   particle imaging, and machine-learning classification of spectra or images.
3. *Validation* — QA/QC: procedural blanks, spike-recovery, reference materials,
   inter-laboratory comparison.

**Core.**
- New or improved methods to prepare, identify or count MPs in water, especially
  small particles; spectral libraries and ML models for polymer identification.
- QA/QC studies and reference materials for MP analysis.
- Drinking-water studies whose methods reach the 1–20 µm range.

**Related.**
- Identification techniques developed in another matrix (tissue, soil, sediment, air)
  that could transfer to water — *borrow*. Sample preparation for another matrix
  (e.g. digesting tissue) usually does not transfer.
- MP concentrations in tap or bottled water measured with standard methods — *cite*
  as reference values.
- Reviews of detection methods; sampling-method comparisons — *cite*.

**Not relevant.**
- Studies that only *use* routine methods to report occurrence outside drinking water.
- Methods only for large particles (> 1 mm), e.g. visual sorting of litter.
- Removing MPs from water (that is P3's topic).

## P2 — Microplastic transport from urban rivers to the estuary

**Goal.** Understand how MPs travel from urban sources through rivers into the
estuary: where they enter, where they settle, and how they break down.

**Aims.**
1. *Sources and inputs* — stormwater runoff, combined sewer overflows,
   wastewater-treatment-plant (WWTP) effluent, tyre-wear particles.
2. *Transport and deposition* — field surveys of water and sediment along rivers,
   lakes and estuaries; settling, resuspension, biofilm effects; transport models.
3. *Weathering and fragmentation* — UV and mechanical breakdown of plastics into MPs.

**Core.**
- Field measurements, experiments or models of MP sources, transport, deposition,
  weathering or fragmentation in rivers, lakes, reservoirs, estuaries and their sediments.

**Related.**
- Coastal or ocean studies, or studies in porous media, whose findings or models could
  carry over to rivers and estuaries — *borrow*.
- Data on MPs in stormwater, WWTP effluent or tyre wear, even when the paper's main
  goal is something else — *cite* as input data.
- Macroplastic deposition in rivers; sampling-method comparisons; reviews of
  freshwater MP occurrence — *cite*.

**Not relevant.**
- MPs in soil or air with no link to waters.
- Drinking water.

## P3 — Capturing microplastics from water with biochar

**Goal.** Develop low-cost biochar filters and adsorbents that capture MPs and
nanoplastics from water, and test them where MPs enter rivers.

**Aims.**
1. *Materials* — biochar feedstock and pyrolysis conditions; surface modification
   (e.g. iron/magnetic loading, oxidation) to improve capture.
2. *Performance and mechanisms* — removal efficiency across particle size, shape and
   polymer; capture mechanisms (electrostatic attraction, pore trapping, π–π
   interactions); regeneration and reuse.
3. *Deployment* — biochar in stormwater filters, bioretention cells, wastewater
   effluent polishing and water-treatment columns.

**Core.**
- Biochar (including modified, magnetic or composite biochar) used to remove MPs or
  nanoplastics from water, at lab, column or field scale.

**Related.**
- Other ways to capture MPs from water — chitosan, cellulose, charcoal or sand filters,
  coagulants, membranes — *compare* against biochar.
- Reviews of MP removal or of carbon-based adsorbents — *cite*.
- Realistic test or reference particles that could be used to test capture — *borrow*.

**Not relevant.**
- Biochar used for other contaminants (metals, nutrients, antibiotics), even when MPs
  are present in the experiment.
- Biochar as a soil amendment that offsets MP effects on soil or crops.
- Biochar as a filler inside plastics; breaking plastics down with enzymes or microbes.
